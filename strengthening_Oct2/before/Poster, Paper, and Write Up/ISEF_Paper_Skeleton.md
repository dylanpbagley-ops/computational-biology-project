# Part 0 — Glossary: every technical term, defined once

Read this section like a dictionary — not necessarily front to back, but come back to it whenever a term below shows up later and you don't remember what it means. Terms are grouped by category. Nothing here is optional background; every one of these terms appears in the results and you'll need to explain at least the biology and statistics ones in your own paper.

## 0.1 The biology of the problem

**Antigen.** A protein (or other molecule) sitting on the outside of a cell that an immune therapy can be designed to recognize and bind to. "Targeting an antigen" means building a drug that specifically grabs onto that one protein.

**On-target toxicity.** Harm caused by a drug doing exactly what it was designed to do — killing cells that carry the antigen — when some of those cells turn out to be normal, healthy cells the patient needs, not just tumor cells. This is different from "off-target" toxicity, where a drug accidentally hits something it wasn't designed to bind at all. Every toxicity discussed in this project is on-target: the drug worked as designed, and that was the problem.

**Therapeutic index / therapeutic window.** How much of a gap exists between "enough drug to kill the tumor" and "so much drug (or so potent a drug) that it also damages normal tissue." A wide therapeutic index means you can dose aggressively and still spare the patient; a narrow one means there's little room to work with. This project's central technical contribution is a way to put a number on this gap for antigen-targeted therapies, before a drug is ever built.

**CAR-T (chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy).** A cancer treatment where a patient's own immune T-cells are genetically engineered to carry a receptor (the CAR) that recognizes a specific antigen on tumor cells, then infused back into the patient to hunt and kill any cell displaying that antigen. Because the T-cell kills any cell with the antigen above some threshold amount, it cannot distinguish "this cell is a tumor cell" from "this cell is a normal cell that happens to carry the same antigen."

**ADC (antibody-drug conjugate).** A cancer drug made of an antibody (which recognizes a specific antigen, like a CAR does) chemically linked to a toxic payload. When the antibody binds a cell carrying the antigen, the payload is released and kills that cell. Functionally, for the purposes of this project, an ADC has the same on-target-toxicity problem as a CAR-T: it cannot tell tumor and normal cells apart if they both carry the antigen.

**Bispecific antibody / T-cell engager (TCE).** A drug that physically links a T-cell to a tumor cell by binding an antigen on the tumor with one arm and a T-cell receptor component with the other, forcing the T-cell to kill the tumor cell it's now attached to. Same on-target-toxicity logic applies.

**Extracellular vesicle (EV).** A tiny particle (roughly 30–150 nanometers across — for scale, about 1,000 times smaller than the width of a human hair) that cells release into blood and other body fluids, carrying proteins from the cell that made it on its surface. Because tumor cells release EVs carrying tumor-associated proteins, EVs in a blood sample can be used as a "liquid biopsy" — a way to detect cancer without a surgical biopsy, by fishing tumor EVs out of blood using antibodies that grab their surface proteins. This is the technology this project's earliest work (Aims 1–9) was built around, before the project pivoted to ask the same underlying mathematical question about a different clinical setting (CAR-T/ADC target selection instead of EV capture).

**Affinity capture.** The general process of using an antibody or receptor coated on a surface to selectively grab a target molecule (or an EV carrying it) out of a mixture, based on binding affinity. The EV-capture work models this process mathematically.

**Confinement (this project's term).** How narrowly an antigen's expression is concentrated in just one or a few normal cell types across the whole body, versus being spread thinly across many. A confined antigen is expressed in a small number of specific normal cell types; a broadly expressed one shows up almost everywhere. This is the project's central biological measurement — see Part 0.3 below for the precise mathematical definitions (`top_share` and `n_types`).

**Expendable lineage.** A population of normal cells the body can lose without permanent harm, either because it regenerates continuously from stem cells (like blood cells, which regenerate from bone marrow) or because medicine already has an established replacement for its function (like immunoglobulin replacement therapy for lost antibody-producing B cells). Blood cell types (B cells, plasma cells, and other white blood cell lineages) are the standard example of an expendable lineage in this project. A solid-tissue cell type — like the cells lining your gut, cornea, or lung — is generally not expendable: there's no straightforward medical replacement if you destroy it.

**Haematopoietic.** Related to the blood-cell-forming system — bone marrow and the blood cell lineages it produces (B cells, T cells, plasma cells, neutrophils, and so on). This project uses "haematopoietic home cell type" as its specific, checkable definition of "expendable" (see 0.3).

**RNA-seq / single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq).** A technology that measures which genes are turned on ("expressed") in a cell by counting the RNA molecules ("transcripts") that gene produces, one cell at a time. RNA is the intermediate molecule a cell makes from its DNA before building a protein, so RNA level is a proxy for — but not identical to — the protein level actually present at the cell surface (this distinction matters a great deal in this project's Discussion/Limitations; see §26, §32 in the results doc).

**CITE-seq.** A technology that measures both RNA (as above) and, simultaneously in the same single cells, specific surface proteins — using antibodies tagged with a DNA barcode instead of a fluorescent dye, so protein levels can be counted the same way RNA is counted. This gives a genuine single-cell protein reference, not just an RNA proxy.

**CyTOF (mass cytometry).** A single-cell technology that measures many proteins on the surface of individual cells at once (dozens per cell) using antibodies tagged with heavy metal isotopes, detected by a mass spectrometer instead of a fluorescence detector. This is the technology behind the project's main DLBCL (lymphoma) patient cohort.

**Human Protein Atlas (HPA).** A public scientific reference database that has systematically measured gene and protein expression across essentially every normal human tissue and cell type, using multiple independent technologies (RNA sequencing, antibody staining, mass spectrometry). This project uses several different HPA data files as its "what does a normal, healthy body look like" reference — the normal-tissue backbone the whole confinement measurement rests on.

**Gating.** In single-cell data, the process of using measured markers to sort a mixed population of cells (e.g., a tumor biopsy full of tumor cells, T cells, and immune cells all mixed together) into the specific cell populations you actually want to analyze (e.g., "just the tumor B cells" or "just the normal T cells"). Every comparison in this project between "tumor" and "normal" cells depends on gating being done correctly and consistently.

## 0.2 The statistics

**AUC (area under the ROC curve).** A single number between 0 and 1 that measures how well a measurement separates two groups. An AUC of 1.0 means the two groups never overlap at all — you could always tell them apart perfectly using this one measurement. An AUC of 0.5 means the measurement is no better than a coin flip. This project mostly calls this quantity "separation." Equivalently (and this is the definition the project's own code actually uses): AUC = the probability that a randomly chosen member of group A has a higher value than a randomly chosen member of group B (counting exact ties as half a point each way). Practically: if AUC(tumor cells' antigen count vs. normal cells' antigen count) = 0.98, that antigen almost perfectly distinguishes tumor from normal on this measurement.

**ROC curve (receiver operating characteristic curve).** A plot that shows, for every possible decision threshold you could set (e.g., "kill any cell with more than X copies of the antigen"), the trade-off between how much tumor you kill (true positive rate / sensitivity) and how much normal tissue you kill (false positive rate). The AUC is just the area underneath this curve. A therapy's entire menu of possible trade-offs, for a given antigen and a given kill-rule shape, is captured by one ROC curve.

**Convex hull.** Imagine plotting a scatter of points on a graph, then stretching a rubber band around all of them — the outer boundary the rubber band settles into is the convex hull. In this project's theorem, "the achievable outcomes lie in the convex hull of the ROC curve" means: no matter how you engineer the therapy, the best (tumor killed, harm) pairs you can achieve are bounded by that rubber-band boundary around the ROC curve — you cannot get an outcome that lies outside it.

**Monotonic / monotone increasing.** A function that only goes up (or stays flat), never down, as its input increases. This project's central theorem requires that a therapy's kill probability rises (or at least never falls) as the amount of antigen on a cell increases — i.e., "more antigen on this cell means the drug is at least as likely to kill it, never less likely." This is a reasonable assumption for how CAR-T and ADCs are believed to work, and the project explicitly discusses the one place in its own data where this assumption is measurably violated (a saturation/non-monotonic effect found in a claudin-18.2 pancreatic cell line calibration).

**Neyman–Pearson lemma.** A classical, foundational result in statistics that says: if you want the best possible trade-off between two kinds of "getting it right" (like tumor killed vs. normal spared), the mathematically optimal rule is always to threshold the *likelihood ratio* — the ratio of how likely a given measurement is under one group versus the other. No other decision rule can do strictly better on both counts. This project's whole ROC-ceiling theorem builds directly on this: it says the achievable region for any antigen-based kill-rule is exactly the region this lemma predicts, whether the "kill rule" was designed with that lemma in mind or not.

**Permutation test / permutation p-value.** A way to test whether an observed difference between two groups (e.g., safe targets vs. toxic targets) could plausibly have arisen by pure chance, without assuming any particular statistical distribution. The method: shuffle the group labels randomly many times (or, when the sample is small enough, enumerate every possible way the labels could have been assigned), recompute the statistic (like AUC/separation) each time, and see what fraction of those random shufflings produce a result at least as extreme as the one actually observed. That fraction is the p-value. An "exact" permutation p-value means every possible shuffling was checked (feasible only for small samples); an "approximate" one means a large random sample of shufflings was used instead because checking every one would take too long.

**p-value.** The probability, if there were truly no real effect at all (pure chance), of seeing a result at least as extreme as the one you actually observed. A small p-value (conventionally below 0.05) is read as evidence the effect is unlikely to be pure chance — but a p-value is not the probability the finding is true, and this project is careful never to claim that.

**Leave-one-out analysis.** A way to check that a finding doesn't secretly depend on just one or two unusual data points. The method: drop one data point (e.g., one target gene) from the analysis, rerun the whole calculation, put that point back, drop the next one, and repeat for every single point. If the result stays essentially the same no matter which point you drop, the finding is described as "robust" — not an accident of one outlier.

**Spearman correlation (Spearman's rho, ρ).** A number between −1 and 1 measuring how consistently two rankings agree, without assuming the relationship between them is a straight line — it only cares about whether "higher in A" tends to go with "higher in B." A value of 1.0 means the two rankings agree perfectly; 0 means no relationship; −1 means they're perfectly reversed. This project uses it throughout to check whether two independent measurements of the same thing (e.g., two different normal-tissue reference datasets) agree with each other.

**Youden's J statistic.** A single number (sensitivity + specificity − 1, ranging from −1 to 1) summarizing how good a yes/no decision rule is at its best possible threshold. 0 means the rule is no better than chance; 1 means it's a perfect separator. This project uses it when comparing whether a two-antigen combination rule beats the best possible single-antigen rule.

**Null control / negative control.** A test built specifically to fail (to show "no effect") when everything is working correctly, so it can catch a hidden bug or a spurious result. This project uses two kinds extensively: (1) a shuffled-label null, where the same analysis pipeline is run on the same data but with the tumor/normal (or safe/toxic) labels randomly scrambled — since any real biological signal has been destroyed by the scrambling, whatever result survives is the "floor" produced by fitting alone, not biology; (2) a specificity control, where the pipeline is applied to a comparison it should NOT be able to solve if it's measuring what it claims to measure (see the efficacy_fail vs. toxic comparison in §23.4 for a worked example, including an honest case where this control didn't behave exactly as predicted).

**Pre-registration.** The practice of writing down, publicly and with a timestamp, exactly what you predict a future analysis will find — before you actually run that analysis or see its result. This guards against "after-the-fact" reasoning, where a scientist unconsciously (or consciously) shapes their story to fit whatever the data happened to show. This project has used OSF (Open Science Framework, a nonprofit platform for this exact purpose) for two separate things: publicly timestamping a file of locked predictions before checking them, and a fuller formal pre-registration of an analysis plan.

## 0.3 This project's own defined measurements

**`top_share`.** The project's primary confinement measurement. For a given gene/antigen, look at its expression level across every normal human cell type in a reference dataset; `top_share` is the fraction of the gene's total expression (summed across all cell types) that is concentrated in just its single highest-expressing cell type. A value near 1.0 means the antigen is almost entirely restricted to one cell type (highly confined); a value near 1/(number of cell types) means it's spread almost perfectly evenly everywhere (not confined at all).

**`n_types`.** A second, related confinement measurement: the number of normal cell types in which the antigen is expressed above 10% of its own maximum expression level (i.e., a "meaningful presence" cutoff relative to its own peak). Fewer cell types means more confined. `top_share` and `n_types` usually agree with each other (they're both measuring the same underlying idea) but are computed differently, so the project reports both as a form of cross-check.

**The two-factor rule (confined AND expendable).** The project's central, no-fitted-parameter decision rule for whether a target should be safe: a target is predicted safe only if it is BOTH confined to few normal cell types AND its highest-expressing ("home") cell type is an expendable/haematopoietic lineage. Implemented, very concretely, as: score = `top_share`, plus 1.0 added on top if the home cell type is haematopoietic. Because no confinement value (which maxes out at 1.0) can ever be as large as the +1.0 bonus, this scoring rule always ranks "any confined-or-not target with an expendable home" above "any target, however confined, with a non-expendable home" — this is why the project calls it "lexicographic" (like alphabetizing: the first letter always wins before you even look at the second).

**The ceiling / ROC ceiling.** The mathematical upper bound on how good the trade-off between tumor-killing and normal-tissue-sparing can ever be for a given antigen, computed directly from how that antigen's amount is distributed in tumor cells versus normal cells, before any specific drug is built. This is a *necessary* condition (a target that fails this test is provably bad) but not a *sufficient* one (passing it doesn't guarantee clinical success, since many other things can still go wrong — see the honest-limitations material throughout).

**n\* (n-star).** The specific antigen-density threshold, expressed in molecules per cell, at which a given real drug's or CAR construct's kill probability crosses 50% (or whatever threshold is specified). This is a real, physically measurable number for a real drug — the project has three real, independently measured examples on file (CD19-directed constructs at roughly 81 and 843 molecules/cell for two different agents, and a BCMA-directed agent at roughly 100 molecules/cell). Engineering choices like antibody affinity or receptor density move a drug's n\* left or right — i.e., they move where on the ROC curve a real drug operates — but they cannot move the ROC curve itself.

**Escape fraction.** The fraction of a cell population (tumor or normal) that survives a given therapy — i.e., the population's antigen count falls below the drug's activation threshold n\*, so the drug's kill probability for those cells is low. This equals exactly the population's cumulative distribution function (the fraction of the population below a given value) evaluated at n\* — a fact that turns out to have real mathematical consequences (see the Methods skeleton for the exact derivation).

**Restriction (as used in the healthy-donor PBMC results, §16).** Nearly the same concept as confinement/`top_share`, but computed specifically on a healthy-donor reference (rather than a broad HPA reference): the largest AUC separating any one healthy immune-cell lineage from all the others, for a given surface protein.

# Part 1 — The whole story in plain English, before the sections

Read this part like you'd explain the project to a smart friend who knows no biology and no statistics. Get comfortable telling this version out loud before you try to write the formal version — if you can't tell this story from memory, the formal paper will read as copied rather than understood, and a judge will find the gap in the interview.

**The problem the field has.** When doctors design a targeted cancer therapy — a CAR-T cell, an antibody-drug conjugate, a bispecific antibody — they pick a protein (an antigen) that sits on the surface of tumor cells, and build a drug that kills any cell carrying that protein. The problem is that almost no protein exists only on tumor cells. Most of the proteins doctors have tried targeting also sit on some normal, healthy cells somewhere in the body — and the drug can't tell the difference. It just kills whatever carries the antigen, tumor or not. Sometimes that's fine (the normal cells that get hit are ones the body can spare or regrow). Sometimes it's catastrophic (patients have died from these drugs attacking essential normal tissue). The field's standard practice for picking a target is to look for an antigen that's much higher on the tumor than on normal tissue — a "good fold-change." This project's whole argument is that fold-change is close to the wrong question, and that the right question can be answered with normal-tissue data alone, before a single patient is ever dosed.

**The project's own origin story (Aims 1–9).** The project actually started somewhere else — building a mathematical model of how "affinity capture" (using antibody-coated surfaces to fish tumor-derived extracellular vesicles out of a blood sample, for early cancer detection) depends nonlinearly on how many receptor molecules sit on a vesicle's surface. Working through that model on real pancreatic-cancer data (comparing the antigens CA19-9 and EGFR on tumor cells, PANC-1, against normal pancreatic cells, HPDEC) produced the project's first big surprise: ranking targets by a simple average expression level gives a different, and wrong, answer than ranking them by the model's more careful accounting of how the *whole distribution* of receptor counts behaves — a "ranking flip." That was the seed of everything that followed: the idea that the *shape* of how much antigen is where matters more than a single average number.

**The pivot, and the bigger question.** The project then pivoted from "how do you build a good liquid-biopsy diagnostic" to "how do you build a good targeted cancer therapy" — same underlying mathematical DNA (a nonlinear function of receptor/antigen count), applied to a clinical question with much higher stakes and much better data available: real single-cell measurements from actual cancer patients, and real clinical safety records for real approved and failed drugs. Working with real lymphoma patient data (CyTOF measurements on 41 real DLBCL patients), the project derived an exact mathematical fact: the fraction of a cell population that "escapes" a drug (survives because it has too little antigen to trigger a strong kill) is exactly a statistical quantity called a cumulative distribution function, evaluated at the drug's own antigen-density activation threshold. That fact turns out to unlock everything else, because it means the entire menu of possible trade-offs a drug could ever achieve against a given antigen — no matter how the drug's specific engineering (antibody affinity, receptor density, and so on) is tuned — is bounded by a single mathematical object: the ROC curve of the antigen's distribution in tumor cells versus normal cells. Engineering can move you along that curve. It cannot move the curve itself.

**The prediction this makes, and the test that mattered most.** If a target's whole achievable safety ceiling is set by how its antigen is distributed in normal tissue, then you should be able to predict, from normal-tissue data ALONE — with no cancer data at all, no drug data at all — which real antigens turned out to have safe clinical records and which turned out to cause severe toxicity. The project built exactly that test, using a public reference of normal human tissue covering 154 different normal cell types across the whole body, and a hand-curated, citation-backed list of 44 real clinical targets with a known safety verdict (19 with a documented safe/tolerable record, 11 with documented severe on-target toxicity, and a further 12 that failed for other reasons — lack of efficacy, not toxicity — used as a separate check rather than the headline test). The result: how confined an antigen is to a small number of normal cell types, combined with whether that cell type is one the body can spare (like blood cells), separates the safe targets from the toxic ones almost perfectly — and this was computed using nothing but public, pre-existing normal-tissue data, with no patient outcome data anywhere near the calculation until the very end, when the prediction was checked against the real clinical record.

**Why this is more than a correlation.** A skeptical reader's first reaction is usually "sure, but confined-to-blood-cells and safe probably just both track something else, like fold-change, or how hard people tried to develop the drug." The project checks and rules out exactly these alternative explanations: a target's confinement predicts safety even when blood-cancer targets are removed entirely (so it isn't a blood-specific artifact); it replicates on a second, completely independent normal-tissue reference and at the protein level, not just RNA; it replicates in an entirely separate solid-tumor dataset from an independent laboratory with a matched-normal-tissue design; and it does NOT predict something it shouldn't (whether a drug program advanced into late-stage trials) while it DOES predict something it should (on-target toxicity) — checked against a registry of clinical trials compiled by an entirely different research group. The project also ran the same calculation across every gene in the human genome and asked whether it independently rediscovers real drug targets nobody told it about — it recovered 10 of 16 antigens already in active clinical development, a 57-fold enrichment over chance.

**What this changes, stated as the thesis.** The reason certain famous cancer drugs work is not that their target is uniquely "tumor-specific" — most of the targets that became approved drugs are not, in fact, cleanly tumor-specific. The reason they work is that their target happens to live almost exclusively on a normal cell population the body can afford to lose. Target selection has, in effect, been solved for blood cancers by accident (because blood cell lineages are unusually confined and unusually expendable) — and the same accident has not been available in solid tumors, because solid tissues generally don't have an expendable "spare" lineage the way blood does. This reframes what "finding a good target" even means: it is not primarily a search for tumor specificity, it is a search for a normal-tissue home the body can do without.

**The honest parts, which belong in the paper as much as the wins.** Several real mistakes were made and caught during this work, and the paper should say so rather than hide it — a substring-matching bug that briefly inflated a result, a wrong diagnosis of an unrelated discrepancy, a specificity control that stopped cleanly passing once the target panel grew (and was investigated rather than ignored), a documented case (CEACAM5) where confinement alone is not enough and the "confined AND expendable" combined rule is needed instead, and a clearly stated boundary case (a saturating, non-monotonic drug-response curve measured in this project's own data) that violates the theorem's core assumption. A judge respects a project that reports what didn't work at least as much as one that only reports wins — arguably more, because it's the strongest signal that the numbers can be trusted.

# Part 2 — What the actual paper needs to contain, and why this structure

Two important, slightly different things get called "the paper" in this project's own documents, and it's worth being precise about which one you're actually going to submit.

**What's officially required for judging (per AzSEF/ISEF rules, confirmed by looking this up directly):** Regeneron ISEF itself does not strictly require a full traditional research paper — the officially required written deliverable is a 250-word Abstract, plus a "Project Presentation" document with these sections: **Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusions, References** (References capped at one page). AzSEF (Arizona's regional fair, which is your path to ISEF) uses this same structure. A fuller, more traditional "research paper" is optional — judges aren't required to read it if you submit one — but writing one is still valuable, both because some judges do read it and value the extra rigor, and because writing the fuller version forces you to actually work through and understand everything well enough to defend it live, which is what matters most in the judging interview itself.

**Practical decision, and why this skeleton is built the way it is:** rather than write two separate documents, this skeleton is built around the required six-section structure (Abstract, Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusions, References), but treats each section with the depth a fuller paper would need. That way, the document you actually produce satisfies the official requirement AND functions as the complete paper "Poster, Paper, and Write Up" already assumes exists. If your poster needs its own separate, shorter distillation later, that's a distillation of this document, not a new writing project.

**Section order to actually write in — NOT the order to read the material in.** You should write in the order: Methods → Results → Discussion → Conclusion → Introduction → Abstract. The Introduction and Abstract are much easier to write well once the rest exists, because they're both summaries of decisions you'll only really have made by the time you've written the Results and Discussion. Trying to write the Introduction first often means rewriting it completely once you see what your Results section actually turned into. (This skeleton still presents them in reading order — Abstract, Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion — because that's the order a judge reads them in; just know that the order you draft them in, section by section together, doesn't have to match.)

## 3.1 Introduction

**Purpose.** Establish the problem, why it matters, what the field currently does about it and why that's insufficient, and state your thesis — all before you've shown a single result. A judge should finish your Introduction able to say, in one sentence, what question you're answering and why the answer isn't already known.

**Suggested flow, in order:**

1. **Open with the clinical stakes, concretely.** Antigen-targeted therapies (CAR-T, ADCs, bispecifics — define these, see glossary 0.1) have produced some of the biggest recent wins in cancer treatment, but on-target/off-tumor toxicity has also caused real, sometimes fatal, patient harm. Concrete, real, citable examples from this project's own label file: a HER2-directed CAR-T caused a fatal pulmonary event within hours of infusion (the target was also expressed in normal lung tissue); FOLR1/mirvetuximab carries an FDA boxed warning for on-target ocular toxicity despite being an approved drug; tisotumab vedotin (targeting tissue factor/F3) carries a boxed warning for ocular toxicity affecting up to 60% of patients. These are real, named, sourced cases — use them (with their full evidence as recorded in `target_labels.py` / `Label_Review_Sheet.md`) rather than a vague statement that "toxicity happens."
2. **State the field's current standard practice, and its limitation.** Target selection today is driven mainly by looking for antigens elevated on tumor cells relative to normal tissue — a fold-change or differential-expression approach. Explain, in your own words, why this doesn't automatically predict whether the *normal* expression that does exist will be dangerous: it treats "how much lower on normal tissue" as the only thing that matters, without asking *which* normal cells carry it or whether the body can spare them.
3. **State what's genuinely missing from the field, backed by the novelty check this project actually did** (§7 and §27.4/discussion material for prior art). Name the closest prior work honestly: single-cell/RNA-based target-prioritization pipelines (e.g., SCAN-ACT, Dannenfelser et al.'s Boolean logic-gate screen), a 2025 bioRxiv logic-gate paper that explicitly states it "did not distinguish between tumor-targeting and tumor-killing efficacy," and a 2025 Frontiers in Immunology review of CAR-T mathematical models that confirms no reviewed model treats antigen density as a distribution yielding a computable ceiling, or offers a general antigen-ranking framework based on it. A PubMed search for "Neyman-Pearson" or "likelihood-ratio" combined with "immunotherapy target discrimination" returns zero results. State plainly: nobody has published a way to compute, from normal-tissue data alone, an upper bound on how safe any therapy against a given antigen could ever be.
4. **State your thesis/hypothesis directly.** In your own words, something like: "the achievable trade-off between tumor-killing and normal-tissue harm for any antigen-targeted therapy is mathematically bounded by how that antigen is distributed across normal cell types in the body — and this bound is computable in advance, from public normal-tissue reference data, without any tumor or outcome data at all." Then state the specific, falsifiable prediction that follows from it and that the paper goes on to test: that a measurable property of an antigen's normal-tissue distribution (how confined it is, and whether its home cell type is expendable) should separate real clinical targets with safe records from ones with documented toxicity — computed from normal-tissue data collected with zero knowledge of which targets succeeded or failed clinically.
5. **One sentence on where the idea came from**, briefly — the project's earlier work modeling how affinity-based capture of tumor-derived extracellular vesicles depends nonlinearly on antigen/receptor count (Aims 1–9), and the ranking-flip finding on pancreatic cancer markers (CA19-9 vs. EGFR) that first showed a naive average can rank targets in the wrong order once the whole distribution is accounted for. This doesn't need to be more than a paragraph; it's a bridge, not the headline.

**What you need to write:** everything above, in your own words and your own sentence structures — Claude will not supply sentences for you to lightly edit. Use the section-by-section loop to test whether your draft actually makes the stakes and the gap clear to someone who's never heard of this project.

**What NOT to do here:** don't front-load statistics or numbers from the Results section. The Introduction motivates the question; it doesn't answer it.

## 3.2 Methods

**Purpose.** Explain precisely enough that someone else could, in principle, redo your analysis: what data you used, what you measured, how you measured it, and what statistical tests you applied and why. This is the section where full, careful definitions matter most, because a judge reading only the Methods section (before seeing any results) needs to understand exactly what a number like "separation = 0.914" is going to mean before they get to it.

**Suggested flow, in order:**

### 3.2.1 The theoretical model (what makes a therapy's outcome mathematically bounded)

Explain, building from the glossary above:
- What a kill rule is (a function of antigen count on a cell, giving the probability that cell is killed), and why it's reasonable to assume it's monotonic (glossary 0.2) — more antigen means at least as likely to be killed, never less.
- The exact identity the project derived: for a specific mathematical form of kill probability (`theta(n) = z·q / (1 + z·q)`, where `q = exp(n·σ·K) − 1`, and `n` is antigen count per cell), the fraction of any population escaping the drug below some threshold `tau` is exactly `F(n*)` — the population's cumulative distribution function evaluated at one specific point, `n* = ln(1 + tau/(z·(1-tau))) / (σ·K)`. Explain in your own words why this matters: it collapses what looked like a whole grid of engineering parameters into one number that has direct physical meaning (an activation threshold, in molecules per cell) and that has already been measured for real drugs.
- The theorem itself, stated in plain language first, then (if you want, and it's a strong move for a math/bio hybrid project) in its precise form: for ANY monotone kill rule, the achievable (tumor killed, harm) points are bounded by the convex hull of the ROC curve of the tumor antigen distribution versus a properly weighted mixture of every normal cell type, where each cell type's weight reflects how much the body needs it (`w_c`, the expendability weight, 0 for fully expendable, 1 for essential). ### 3.3.2 The prediction the theorem implies, and the central test (results doc §23)

This is the project's most important empirical result, and deserves the most careful, complete write-up.

**State the prediction first, before any number**, exactly the way the project itself insists on doing it: if the theorem is right, then a target's confinement across normal cell types, combined with whether its home cell type is expendable, should predict real clinical safety outcomes — using only public normal-tissue data, with zero tumor or outcome information going into the measurement.

**Then the panel** (Methods 3.2.3 covers how it was built; here, state its final composition): 44 targets — 19 `safe`, 11 `toxic`, 12 `efficacy_fail` (used only for the specificity check in 3.3.2f below), 2 excluded. Note, honestly, that the panel grew over the course of the project (40 → 42 → 44 targets) as further literature review turned up additional targets with a settled clinical verdict, and that every addition is independently sourced and cited — this is worth stating because it shows the panel wasn't fixed arbitrarily at a size that happened to produce a good result; growth was driven by literature availability, not by result-shopping (and in fact one specific control result got *harder* to defend as the panel grew — see 3.3.2f — which is itself evidence the process wasn't being steered toward convenient results).

**a) Confinement alone** (§23.1): `top_share` separates 19 safe from 11 toxic targets at **separation 0.880, permutation p = 0.00014**; `n_types` gives essentially the same answer (0.876, p = 0.00018). Illustrate with the concrete ladder of real examples the results document gives: CD20 (0.850, 1 cell type, approved) → BCMA (0.789, 1, approved) → PSMA (0.568, 2, approved) → claudin-18.2 (0.359, 8, approved 2024) → CEA/CEACAM5 (0.283, 7, caused severe colitis) → tissue factor/F3 (0.075, 31, boxed-warning ocular toxicity) → EGFR (0.051, 60) → HER2 (0.041, 72, a fatal on-target CAR-T event) → CD47 (0.030, 120, causes on-target anemia). This ladder is worth including close to verbatim (with your own framing sentences) because it's the single clearest illustration of the whole thesis: confinement drops, toxicity risk rises, in a real, checkable, named sequence.

**b) The exception that motivates the two-factor rule** (§23.2): CEACAM5 (CEA) is confined (7 cell types, essentially colonocytes) and CEA-directed CAR-T still caused severe colitis — pure confinement alone doesn't fully explain safety. State this directly as a real counterexample your project found and did not paper over. Then introduce the two-factor rule (confined AND expendable — Methods 3.2.2) as the fix, and report: **two-factor separation 0.914, permutation p = 0.00003** — an improvement over confinement alone (0.880). Walk through why the rule explains the exceptions correctly: CD19/CD20 → B cells (confined + expendable) → approved; claudin-18.2 → gastric mucosa (confined + regenerating) → approved; CEACAM5 → colonocytes (confined, NOT expendable) → colitis; HER2/CD47 → expressed nearly everywhere (not confined at all) → catastrophic. **Also report the bug found and fixed here** (§23.2): an early version of the "is this cell type haematopoietic" check used simple substring matching, which incorrectly classified "conjunctival goblet cells" as blood-lineage because the string contains "t cell" — inflating the two-factor separation to 0.916. The bug was fixed (word-boundary matching, with a permanent automated guard against it recurring) and the number reported throughout is the corrected, smaller 0.914/0.905-range value, not the inflated one. This is exactly the kind of self-caught-error disclosure a judge respects.

**c) Leave-one-out robustness** (§23.3): dropping each of the 30 safe-or-toxic targets one at a time and rerunning, separation stays between 0.905 and 0.947, and remains statistically significant (p < 0.05) in all 30 of 30 drops. No single target is carrying the result.

**d) Sensitivity check: removing germ/placental tissue** (§23.5): several targets have their single highest-expressing normal cell type in immune-privileged reproductive tissue (e.g., a target whose top hit is sperm precursor cells) — sites that are biologically shielded from immune attack in ways that wouldn't show up as toxicity even if the raw confinement number suggested risk. Removing all reproductive/placental cell types from the reference and recomputing barely changes anything (0.880 → 0.856 for confinement alone; the two-factor rule is unchanged at 0.914) — and the cell-type reassignments that do happen are corrective, not damaging (one target's assigned home cell type moves from an obscure reproductive tissue to B cells, which is where its real drug actually acts).

**e) The load-bearing check: solid tumors only, with every blood-cancer target removed** (§23.6). This directly answers the most obvious skeptical objection — "doesn't this only work because blood targets happen to be both confined and safe?" With every blood-cancer target excluded (8 safe solid-tumor targets vs. 10 toxic solid-tumor targets remaining): **separation 0.812 (`top_share`), exact p = 0.0133.** The finding survives, and is not a blood-cancer artifact.

**f) The specificity control, and the honest complication** (§23.4 and §34/Sixth addendum — report this fully and honestly, not glossed over). The idea: if confinement merely tracked "this drug program failed for any reason" rather than specifically "this caused toxicity," then targets that failed for lack of *efficacy* (not safety) should look like the toxic group too — and they shouldn't, if the measure is doing what it claims. As originally run (on a 40-target panel), this control passed cleanly: efficacy-failure targets scored near chance relative to safe targets, not like the toxic group. **As the panel grew to 44 targets (two additions in particular — ERBB3/HER3 and AXL, both broadly-expressed, low-confinement solid-tumor antigens that failed for a clean, sponsor-confirmed lack of efficacy), this control moved from a borderline, non-significant separation (0.700, p = 0.043) to a small but statistically real one (0.746, p = 0.011).** Report the investigation, not just the number: the efficacy-failure group turns out to split into two real biological clusters — a small immune-lineage subgroup that scores like the safe group, and a larger group of broadly-expressed solid-tumor antigens that scores more like the toxic group — and this split predates the two newest additions; they simply landed on the already-existing low side of it. State the honest interpretation directly: a broadly-expressed, low-confinement antigen may be inherently hard to build any wide therapeutic window around, and whether that shows up in the historical record as "on-target toxicity" or as "failed for lack of efficacy" (because the dose had to be capped for tolerability before efficacy could even be shown) may be two faces of the same underlying limitation — a plausible extension of the project's own thesis, not a contradiction of it. Say plainly that this means the specificity control no longer cleanly demonstrates "confinement says nothing about efficacy failure in general," and that this is now reported as a genuine, disclosed limitation of that one control — while stating equally plainly that it does not touch sections (a)-(e) above, because efficacy-failure targets are never used in any of those tests.

**g) Independent normal-tissue reference** (§23.7): the whole analysis repeated on a second, independent HPA reference (51 whole tissues instead of 154 cell types) reproduces the finding (separation 0.751, p = 0.0118) — weaker, as expected, because whole tissues can't resolve individual cell types as finely, but a real, independent replication. The two references agree with each other at Spearman ρ = 0.592 (p = 0.00002) — correlated but not identical, an honest statement that the two aren't perfectly interchangeable measurement tools.

## 3.4 Discussion
